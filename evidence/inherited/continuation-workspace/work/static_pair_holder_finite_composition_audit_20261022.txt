1. PRECISE LOCAL HYPOTHESES

Use born-source units, lattice spacing xi<=1 and epsilon=L^{-(r-i)}<=1.
Assume xi epsilon is within a fixed small matrix-logarithm radius.
Let Q be a collared local box, or a fixed-complexity branch read with
the following explicit path property: the short contours used in its
covariant norms remain inside the controlled collar and have length
at most C_path |x-y| for |x-y|<=c/epsilon. All finite differences and
their shifted arguments are included in that collar.

Work in ONE frame, with matrix norms in that frame. A connection
U_mu(x)=exp(i xi A_mu(x)) obeys

 |A|<=C epsilon, |D_0 A|<=C epsilon^2,
 [D_0 A]_alpha<=C epsilon^(2+alpha),  0<alpha<1.          (1)

The Holder seminorm is needed on distances up to O(epsilon^-1);
larger distances are bounded using the supremum norms. A claim about
an arbitrary disconnected/nonconvex branch without controlled paths
is not covered. Local application to the complete collared cubes is
enough for the usual branch estimate.

2. EXACT COVARIANT-TO-ORDINARY CONVERSION

B99(3.3) gives, for the adjoint field components used by the native
response,

 D^U_mu H(x)=D^0_mu H(x)+C_mu(x) H(x+xi e_mu),
 C_mu(x)=(Ad U_mu(x)-I)/xi.                              (2)

The same component formula applies to a bond field after the paper's
identification of its directional components. Smoothness of the matrix
exponential and (1) imply, uniformly in xi,

 |C|<=C epsilon, |D_0 C|<=C epsilon^2,
                         [C]_alpha<=C epsilon^(1+alpha). (3)

Suppose |H|<=C epsilon, |D^U H|<=C epsilon^2 and the covariant
Holder seminorm of D^U H is <=C epsilon^(2+alpha), with the native
shortest-contour convention B99(3.40). Equation(2) first gives
|D_0 H|<=C epsilon^2. Combining this bound with |H|<=C epsilon
gives [H]_alpha<=C epsilon^(1+alpha), by the elementary bound
min(2||H||,C||D_0 H|| |x-y|).

There is one more conversion: the assumed Holder norm compares
Ad U(Gamma_xy) D^U H(y) with D^U H(x). For d=|x-y|<=c/epsilon,
(1) and the controlled contour length give

 ||Ad U(Gamma_xy)-I||<=C epsilon d exp(C epsilon d)
                                      <=C' epsilon d.    (4)

This is valid also for small complex connections in the specified
frame. Therefore the ordinary difference of D^U H has the bound

 C epsilon^(2+alpha) d^alpha+C epsilon^3 d
                       <=C' epsilon^(2+alpha) d^alpha.   (5)

For d>c/epsilon the same Holder bound follows from |D^U H|<=C epsilon^2.
Finally the ordinary Holder seminorm of C H_shift in (2) is bounded by

 |C|[H]_alpha+[C]_alpha|H|<=C epsilon^(2+alpha).          (6)

Equations(2),(5),(6) give the required ordinary C1,alpha estimates
for H. This proves the practical covariant version of the target
lemma when its actual intermediate background already has (1).
It does not convert an arbitrary covariant norm with paths outside
the controlled read, or a norm at another determining grade.

3. FINITE PRODUCT AND THE LATTICE CHAIN RULE

For m fixed independently of scales, define the potential of the
pointwise relative product by

 F_xi(A_0,H_1,...,H_m)
   =(i xi)^(-1) log(exp(i xi H_m)...exp(i xi H_1)
                                             exp(i xi A_0)). (7)

Choose the common small chart so the whole finite product remains in
the logarithm neighborhood. F_xi has a removable singularity at xi=0.
On this fixed neighborhood its first derivatives are uniformly bounded
and its second derivatives are O(xi), hence bounded uniformly for
xi<=1. Also F_xi(0)=0. These facts follow directly by the convergent
matrix exponential/logarithm power series (or its finite-dimensional
analytic derivatives). Thus the prefactor xi^-1 does not create an
uncontrolled fine-mesh price.

The discrete chain rule is the exact integral identity

 D^0_mu F_xi(a(x))
   =integral_0^1 DF_xi(a(x)+theta(a(x+xi e_mu)-a(x)))
                                      D^0_mu a(x) dtheta. (8)

It yields the epsilon^2 gradient bound. Subtracting (8) at x and y,
the gradient-difference term costs epsilon^(2+alpha)|x-y|^alpha.
The difference of the Jacobians costs at most
C [a]_alpha |x-y|^alpha times epsilon^2, hence at most
C epsilon^(3+alpha)|x-y|^alpha. The shifted arguments use exactly
the collar in Section1. The value bound follows from F_xi(0)=0.
This proves the original C1,alpha scaling for the finite product.

Starting with (1), Section2 converts the first response from the
covariant norm of its ACTUAL background. Section3 produces the next
controlled background. Repeat only the fixed m times. Constants can
depend on m, alpha, path constants and the stated input amplitudes;
they do not depend on the number of old histories, r-i or xi. No
claim for an unbounded number of response factors follows.

4. WHAT THIS DOES NOT PROVE ABOUT RETURN GAUGES

Covariance of (2) is an algebraic statement. Norm invariance holds
for unitary gauges, but a general complex gauge can have an arbitrarily
large adjoint norm. Hence the following are legitimate sufficient
ways to apply this lemma:

 (a) express the base and all response factors from the outset in one
     native frame on which their required bounds are proved;
 (b) pull them to that frame using actual gauge identities AND bounds
     for the relevant conjugations/transports, retaining the prices;
 (c) prove directly the needed bounds for the composed return gauge.

Independently choosing one good gauge for each minimum is insufficient.
Gauge invariance of F permits evaluating a complete expression in a
chosen representative; it does not supply the bounds required to put
all factors into that representative. The lemma proves none of (a)--(c)
for the literal LII(1.61)--(1.64) return gauges.

In particular it does not prove a global field estimate across a split
interface. The complete branch read, interface geometry and current
profile in any piecewise Ward application remain separate premises.
